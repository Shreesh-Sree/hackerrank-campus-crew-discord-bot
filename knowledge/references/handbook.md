--- Section 5 ---
HackerRank Campus Crew is a global community of students who bring HackerRank's technology, competitions, and career resources directly to their campuses. It exists to close the gap between classroom learning and industry-ready skill, by giving students hands-on access to the same assessment and practice tools used in real technical hiring.



As a Campus Crew Ambassador, you are HackerRank's representative on your campus. You bring coding competitions, hackathons, workshops, and tech talks to your peers, and in doing so, build a technical community that outlasts any single event.



What success looks like:

 Running at least one HackerRank-platform event on your campus every month.

Growing genuine engagement, not just a single one-off event.

Reporting your events and winners promptly so rewards reach students without delay.

Collaborating with other ambassadors and campus communities rather than working in isolation.



Why it matters:

Consistent, well-run events are noticed. Strong ambassadors get priority consideration for HackerRank events and campaigns.

--- Section 7 ---
The ambassador role centers on one goal: growing genuine engagement with HackerRank's platform and technology on your campus. How you achieve that is largely up to you.



Core dimensions of the role:

Representing HackerRank accurately to your campus.

Increasing awareness and platform adoption.

Creating technical opportunities - contests, challenges, and sessions that build real skill.

Building student communities that outlast a single event.

Collaborating with existing clubs rather than competing with them.

Connecting students with industry through resource persons and career content.

Supporting HackerRank initiatives as they launch.

Maintaining an active campus presence between events, not just during them.



Creative freedom:

There is no single required format. A rigorous DSA contest, a casual hackathon, and a beginner-friendly workshop are all valid. Choose formats that fit your campus culture, your strengths, and what your peers are genuinely interested in.

HackerRank for Work (HRW)  Complete Guide

--- Section 17 ---
HRW is the primary platform ambassadors use to run assessments. It is a browser-based, enterprise-grade environment, the same technology used in real technical hiring  with automated evaluation and detailed reporting.



What you can build on HRW?:

DSA and programming tests, ranging from beginner to advanced difficulty.

Multiple-choice question (MCQ) assessments for aptitude or conceptual screening.

Debugging challenges, where participants fix broken code against test cases.

Role-based assessments - Full-Stack, Backend, Frontend, AI/ML Engineer, Data Engineer, DevOps, and more - using a built-in, browser-based coding environment.



Setting up a test:

Log in at hackerrank.com/work/login

Create a new test and choose your question types and difficulty level.

Select or write your questions, and preview each one before publishing.

Set your contest window (start time, duration, and end time) with enough buffer for evaluation afterward.

Lock the test once configured, so it remains private to your account.

Publish and share the contest link - registration happens automatically as participants access the link.

Hackathon-Standard Operating Procedure

--- Section 46 ---
Design decisions:

Theme and tracks: a focused theme gives submissions direction.

Problem statements: open-ended prompts work well for creativity; specific ones work well for judging consistency.

Eligibility: campus-wide, department-specific, or cross-college.

Team formation: solo, fixed size, or open - state this clearly at registration.

Technology restrictions: decide if any tech stack is allowed.



Timeline and rules:

Publish a clear timeline: registration deadline, hacking window, submission deadline, judging/results date.

Define submission requirements: repo link, short write-up, demo video or live demo slot.

State judging criteria up front.



Judging: Judging is the organizing ambassador's responsibility, unless a HackerRank engineer has been arranged as a judge, or the hackathon includes a contest segment that scores automatically.



Judging criteria: Technical execution, Creativity & originality, Problem relevance, Presentation.



A practical structure: Run a focused 1-2 hour HackerRank contest segment as part of the hackathon, rather than fitting the entire flow inside one contest window.



Tie-breaking and results: Decide your tie-breaking rule in advance. Announce winners promptly and move directly into certificates and reward submission.

--- Section 48 ---
Workshops & Bootcamps

--- Section 49 ---
Workshops and bootcamps build a specific skill over one or more sessions - DSA fundamentals, a development stack, AI/ML basics, or interview preparation are all strong topics.



1. Choose a topic scoped to a single session, or a short series (2-3 sessions) for deeper skills.

2. Confirm a speaker - yourself, a team member, a club collaborator, or a requested HackerRank resource person.

3. Draft a simple curriculum or session outline, with a hands-on component wherever possible.

4. Set a duration appropriate to the material - most single sessions run 60-120 minutes.

5. Promote using your standard timeline.

6. Open registration, even if informal, to gauge turnout and plan the venue or online capacity.

7. Run the session with a hands-on activity - ideally one that uses a HackerRank test or practice problem.

8. Collect quick feedback at the end (a short form or a show of hands works fine).

9. Follow up with resources, a recap, and - where a HackerRank contest was used - certificates and results.

Tech Talks & Industry Sessions

--- Section 52 ---
Tech talks connect students with a technical or career topic, and can be delivered by you, another ambassador, a club collaborator, or a HackerRank engineer.



Requesting a HackerRank resource person: For tech talks and hackathon judging, HackerRank can provide an in-house engineer as a speaker or judge. Request this from the Program Manager well in advance of your event date, since availability depends on scheduling - do not announce a HackerRank speaker publicly until it has been confirmed.



Running the session:

Build in dedicated time for Q&A - this is often the most valuable part of the session for students.

Collect feedback and issue certificates where appropriate.

Post a short recap afterward to extend the session's reach beyond the room.

Building Your Campus Team

--- Section 55 ---
Running events solo works, especially early on. As your activity grows, a small team makes execution smoother and more sustainable.



Suggested team structure:

Campus Crew Lead (you): overall ownership, HackerRank liaison, final decisions.

Technical Lead: platform setup, contest configuration, troubleshooting.

Event Operations: logistics, scheduling, on-the-day execution.

Outreach & Marketing: promotion, cross-community reach, social media.

Design & Media: posters, decks, and event visuals.

Community: keeping participants engaged between events.

Documentation: event reports, tracking, record-keeping.



For a single monthly campus event, a small core - a technical lead and an outreach lead alongside yourself - is usually enough. Scale up to the full structure only for larger, multi-college initiatives.



Operating solo?: If you don't have a team, that's fine - many ambassadors run events independently. If your reach is limited in a given month (e.g. exams), partnering with another ambassador for a joint or online event is a strong alternative to skipping the month.

--- Section 57 ---
Building a HackerRank Community

--- Section 58 ---
The most effective ambassadors build a lasting community, not just a sequence of events. A recurring, engaged group of students compounds in value over time - each event becomes easier to promote and better attended than the last.



Growing your community:

Run events on a predictable rhythm (e.g. monthly) so students know what to expect.

Create a simple way for interested students to stay in the loop - a group chat, mailing list, or club channel.

Recognize repeat participants and standout performers publicly.

Keep light-touch engagement going between events - a coding tip, a challenge of the week, or a relevant industry update.



Working with existing clubs: Most campuses already have coding clubs, developer communities, and technical societies. Rather than competing with these for attention, collaborate - co-host events, cross-promote, and share resources. This consistently produces stronger turnout than working in isolation, and avoids unnecessary competition for the same student audience.

Cross-Campus Collaboration

--- Section 61 ---
Ambassadors are encouraged to collaborate across colleges and regions, including internationally, to run joint or larger-scale events. There are no regional boundaries on who you can team up with.



When to collaborate:

Your own campus reach is limited in a given month.

You want to run a larger event than your campus alone can support.

You're organizing an inter-college competition or a themed multi-campus initiative.



Practical guidance:

Agree on ownership early - who manages the platform, who manages promotion, and who is the point of contact with HackerRank.

For international collaborations, agree on a shared time window that reasonably works across time zones.

Keep a single, shared source of truth for the event plan and registration link to avoid duplicated or conflicting information.

Rewards & Benefits

--- Section 64 ---
Winners of ambassador-run events are eligible for the following, based on total participant count:



Fewer than 300 participants:

HackerRank Infinity Plan (1 year), Mock Interview credits, Access to 1,500+ AI tools (6-month access)



300 or more participants:

All of the above, plus HackerRank Merch 

P.S. Merch will be sent to the ambassadors as SPOC, and they can distribute it to the winners later.

Winners are determined by leaderboard ranking on the HackerRank contest.

Structure your winner tiers so rewards feel earned - a clear top-N cutoff works better than an oversized winner list that dilutes the value of the reward.

All participants who complete a submission are eligible for a participation certificate, regardless of ranking.



Welcome kit: New ambassadors receive a shipped welcome kit after onboarding, typically including branded merchandise such as a hoodie, cap or bag, and stickers. Delivery timelines vary by location and current stock - allow several weeks.



HackerRank may also run periodic goody drops tied to active participation in community-wide initiatives, in addition to the standard welcome kit.



Program benefits and requirements may be updated periodically. Confirm the latest reward terms with the Program Manager before announcing them publicly.

--- Section 66 ---
Winner Reward Activation

--- Section 67 ---
HackerRank activates rewards directly for each winner's account once their details are received - ambassadors do not handle or distribute the rewards themselves.



SELECT WINNERS -> VERIFY -> COLLECT DETAILS -> SUBMIT -> ACTIVATION -> WINNER CHECKS -> RESOLVE ISSUES



1. Sort your contest's leaderboard to confirm the final ranking of winners.

2. Verify each winner's identity and HackerRank account email address.

3. Collect each winner's full name and the exact email linked to their HackerRank account.

4. Submit the contest link and winner list to the Program Manager.

5. HackerRank activates the reward directly against each winner's account, typically the same day.

6. Ask winners to check their HackerRank account and inbox (including spam) for confirmation.

7. If a reward is missing, confirm the submitted email matches the winner's actual account, then escalate that specific winner's details to the Program Manager.



Important: always verify that the email you submit matches the winner's actual HackerRank account. A mismatched or personal email is the most common cause of delayed or missing rewards.



Recommended turnaround: Submit winner details within a day or two of your event closing, while participant information is still fresh and easy to verify.

Event Promotion & Marketing

--- Section 73 ---
Strong promotion is often the difference between a well-attended event and an empty one. Use multiple channels rather than relying on a single post.



Channels to use:

College and department groups, and coding/developer club channels.

LinkedIn - the most effective channel for reach, recaps, and winner recognition.

Instagram and other visual platforms, for posters.

Posters and QR codes placed around campus for offline reach.

Classroom outreach and word-of-mouth through fellow students.



Recommended promotion timeline:

T-14 days: Announce the event and open registration.

T-7 days: Second promotion push; reach out to clubs and communities directly.

T-3 days: Reminder post; highlight rewards and what participants will gain.

T-1 day: Final reminder with joining/access details.

Event day: Live updates and encouragement to boost last-minute turnout.

Post-event: Recap, winner announcement, and thank-you to participants and collaborators.



Supporting flagship initiatives: From time to time, HackerRank runs flagship campaigns that ambassadors are invited to support through promotion and participation. Guidance and creative assets are shared by the Program Manager when each initiative launches.

Troubleshooting Manual

--- Section 82 ---
No HRW activation email received

Cause: sent to your application email, may be in spam, or account is in an upcoming onboarding batch.

Check: both inbox and spam/junk for the email you applied with, including college-issued addresses.

Do: use HRC in the meantime so your event isn't delayed. Contact the Technical Lead if it genuinely never arrives.



Contest link returns an error or disappears from the dashboard

Cause: a temporary platform issue, typically during high traffic.

Check: confirm the exact link and whether the contest still appears in your account.

Do: report immediately while the event is live - speed matters for recovery. Contact the Program Manager urgently.



Contest closed before everyone finished

Cause: the contest reached its scheduled end time.

Check: whether the closing time was clearly communicated in advance.

Do: closed contests are not reopened. Build buffer time into future events and report access issues in real time.



Winner hasn't received their reward

Cause: a mismatched email address, or a delay during a high-volume period.

Check: that the submitted email matches the winner's actual HackerRank account.

Do: confirm the email, then follow up with the Program Manager with that specific winner's details.

--- Section 84 ---
Troubleshooting (continued)

--- Section 85 ---
Welcome kit or hamper hasn't arrived

Cause: shipping timelines vary by location and current stock.

Check: how long it has actually been - several weeks is normal.

Do: allow several weeks before following up. If it has been over a month, check in with the Program Manager once.



Another ambassador can see or edit your test

Cause: ambassador accounts share a single Campus Crew workspace.

Check: whether the test was locked immediately after creation.

Do: lock every test immediately after creating it to keep it private to your account.



HRW access received but no SkillUp access yet

Cause: the two platforms are activated separately; SkillUp sometimes follows a few days behind HRW.

Check: how many days have passed since HRW activation.

Do: wait a few days; if SkillUp still hasn't arrived, follow up directly with the Technical Lead.



Participant can't access the contest link

Cause: an incorrect link was shared, or the contest hasn't opened yet.

Check: the link matches exactly what's shown on your admin dashboard, and the contest window has started.

Do: re-share the correct link; if the issue affects multiple participants during a live event, report it immediately to the Program Manager or Technical Lead.

Metrics & Growth

--- Section 91 ---
Tracking the right numbers helps you understand what's working and make the case for bigger, better-supported events over time.



Registrations and participants: your core turnout numbers.

Completion rate: how many registered participants actually submitted.

Departments and years reached: a sign of how broad your audience is, not just how large.

Repeat participants: the clearest signal of a genuinely engaged community.

Club collaborations: how many events were co-hosted rather than run solo.

Event frequency: consistency over time, not just a single strong event.

Social reach: engagement on your promotional and recap posts.



Focus on impact, not vanity numbers: a smaller, highly engaged, repeat audience is worth more to your campus community - and to your own track record - than a one-time spike in registrations.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

--- Section 94 ---
Sharing a contest link before adding any questions to it.

Not testing the platform, questions, or links before publishing.

Using an AI-generated or modified HackerRank logo instead of the official brand asset.

Calling a third-party form or community group \"official HackerRank\" without confirmation.

Promising unconfirmed rewards, resource persons, or judges before they've been arranged.

Submitting a winner's personal email instead of their HackerRank-account email.

Delaying reward submission after an event closes.

Structuring winner tiers so loosely that the reward feels unearned.

Poor documentation - skipping the event report or winner records.

Running events purely to inflate numbers rather than deliver genuine value.

Not having a backup plan for technical issues on event day.

Doing everything alone when a small team or collaboration would work better.

Not following up with winners to confirm they received their rewards.

Best Practices

--- Section 97 ---
Prioritize event quality over headcount - a well-run smaller event builds more trust than a large, poorly organized one.

Invest in your community, not just individual events.

Plan your time realistically, with buffer for both promotion and post-event follow-up.

Build a small, capable team as your activity grows.

Communicate clearly and promptly, both with HackerRank and with participants.

Promote across multiple channels rather than relying on one.

Document consistently - it protects you and makes reporting effortless.

Prioritize participant experience: clear instructions, working links, and fair judging.

Always test the platform yourself before an event goes live.

Submit winner details promptly and follow up until rewards are confirmed received.

Collaborate across campuses when it strengthens the event.

Treat every event as a chance to build your own event-management and leadership skills.

Frequently Asked Questions

--- Section 109 ---
Q: Do I get free access to the 1,500+ AI tools myself, as an ambassador?

A: No - this is a reward for winners of your events, not a personal perk for being an ambassador.



Q: What's the difference between HRW and SkillUp?

A: HRW is what you use to run tests for your campus. SkillUp is what your students use to learn and get certified.



Q: My university isn't listed when creating a contest - what do I do?

A: Select \"Other\" and type your university name manually.



Q: Do I need a separate Google Form for event registrations?

A: Generally no - the HackerRank contest link handles sign-ups automatically. A form can still be useful for outreach.



Q: How is someone counted as a \"participant\" for reward purposes?

A: Submitting an answer or code on the HackerRank test registers someone as a participant.



Q: Can I create certificates myself, or does HackerRank send them?

A: You create and distribute certificates yourself using HackerRank's official templates.



Q: Where do I send my event report and winner list?

A: Directly to the Program Manager, or the Technical Lead for platform-specific matters.



Q: I can't reach many students this month - is that a problem?

A: Team up with another ambassador for a joint or online event rather than skipping the month.

Q: What should I avoid promising participants?
